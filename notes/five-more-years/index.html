Five More Years

November 2026

When we moved to America, we planned to stay for five years. We'd already spent six years in England, and while we were excited about living in the US, we didn't want to spend too long away from our families and friends. Five years seemed long enough to enjoy the experience and make the move worthwhile, but not so long that we'd forget we were supposed to be going back.

For a surprisingly long time, whenever anyone asked when we were returning to Malta, the answer was five years. It didn't matter whether we'd been in Seattle for two years, four years or seven. Then, five years ago, we bought a house. I told Frederika that we would definitely move back five years after buying it. It seemed quite a firm commitment at the time, perhaps because buying a house was the sort of thing that ought to come with a firm commitment.

We've now been in Seattle for about eleven years. We don't say five years anymore.

I left Malta almost twenty years ago, when I was 23 or 24. I don't think I had a particularly sophisticated reason for leaving. Malta is small, and I wanted to see what else was out there. This was an ambition slightly at odds with my travel experience. The first time I left Malta was for a day trip to Sicily in my early twenties, and about a year later I boarded an aeroplane for the first time, to visit Spain with my brother. I apparently liked the idea of the wider world more than I knew anything about it.

There was university too. I'd started an electrical engineering degree, abandoned it after two years and switched to computing. By the second attempt I was a little more mature and, perhaps more importantly, had a lecturer who believed in me. I did well, he eventually became one of my best friends, and I wanted to carry on studying. An MSc was the immediate plan, with a PhD somewhere beyond it. I wasn't thinking very carefully about where all that would lead. I just wanted to keep going.

America was barely a possibility then, though I had family in the Bay Area and liked the idea of living there one day. I ended up in England first. Six years later, Frederika and I moved to Seattle because transferring with Expedia was a relatively easy way into the US. The idea was to move to San Francisco eventually. We'd lived in different places by then, and the prospect of the Bay Area still had some of that promised-land quality I'd attached to it years earlier.

Seattle turned out to be rather nice. We made friends, our careers developed, and moving to San Francisco started to look less like an exciting next step and more like the unnecessary business of starting again. Eventually we bought the house, which was hardly something we'd imagined doing when we arrived for our five-year stay.

I remember telling my brother, when I was about 35, that we were planning to return in five years. "Shit, you'd be 40," he said. He was right, of course. I'd been using five years as a fairly harmless unit of time, without thinking much about what it meant when added to my actual age. I'm 43 now, so his observation has aged better than my prediction.

A few years ago I began thinking more about the life I'd have had if I'd stayed in Malta. Not because I'm unhappy with the one I've had, or because I wish I'd made a different decision. I don't. I'm just curious about what happened in the years I wasn't there. My parents were in their fifties when I left and are now in their seventies. My friends were 19 or in their early twenties; now they're in their forties and most have children. None of this happened suddenly, but when you're living somewhere else you tend to see the changes in visits rather than in everyday increments.

We've been back often, and I've kept close to people, but visiting isn't quite the same as being around. There are all the birthdays and bigger occasions, of course, but it's probably the less memorable things I've missed most: dropping in for coffee, having lunch because you happen to be nearby, or seeing your parents on a completely ordinary Wednesday. I sometimes wonder whether I'd be a substantially different person if I'd stayed. I suspect not, though I might have a different collection of stories and a better idea of which friend's child is which.

I had another odd thought recently about language. After almost twenty years in English-speaking countries, I still have an accent and occasionally find myself missing vocabulary or making grammatical mistakes. But I left Malta young enough that I sometimes wonder whether my Maltese has stayed more or less at the level it was when I was 24. I probably sound slightly stupid in both languages. In practice, I think in whichever language I'm speaking, so perhaps I'm being unfair to myself. I do notice that talking about software comes a little more naturally in English, which isn't surprising given where I've spent most of my working life.

Malta itself hasn't stood still either. It feels like home the moment I arrive, and I've never felt like a tourist there, but there are parts of it I barely recognise. It's busier, much more built up and, in ways that are difficult to describe without sounding like an old man, less Maltese. I sometimes feel as though you can't even order a fucking pastizz in Maltese anymore. There are cranes everywhere, towns have changed beyond recognition, and much of the little countryside we had has been covered in concrete. The politics still manage to irritate me from several thousand miles away.

It would be unfair to pretend everything was better twenty years ago. There are much better restaurants now, nicer bars and more places to go. I also return with more money than I had when I lived there, which probably makes a difference. Back then, putting petrol in my car sometimes meant calculating how much I needed to last until the end of the week, and hoping I hadn't forgotten anywhere I needed to drive.

Frederika sometimes makes me think about how differently things might have turned out. She graduated in mechanical engineering in Slovakia and left just as the 2008 financial crisis was making it difficult to find work. She took a job as a cashier at Zara, then worked her way into finance and eventually into a leadership role. She loves what she does. She can talk about accounting for ages, and I don't think I've ever heard her talk about mechanical engineering with the same enthusiasm.

Then again, perhaps that observation isn't particularly fair. When she first left university, her English wasn't yet good enough for a long conversation about engineering, and by the time it was, she had been working in other fields for years. She'd actually studied accounting before switching to mechanical engineering, so in a roundabout way she ended up back where she started. Would she have been an engineer if she'd stayed in Slovakia? Would she have enjoyed it? I don't know, and neither of us has an alternative version of her career to compare it with.

For a long time, going back to Malta was a vague plan that could comfortably live five years in the future. Lately it's begun to feel much more like something I want to do. The difficulty is that I don't particularly want to move back to work a normal nine-to-five job. That isn't because I think I'm above it, or that there's anything wrong with the lives my friends have built. It's just that after spending so many years away, I'd like the effort of leaving and building careers abroad to have bought us something more than the option of doing the same thing somewhere else.

What I want, I think, is financial independence. I say that quite confidently for someone who hasn't worked out exactly what it means or how much money it requires. I don't want to stop doing things. I enjoy software development, and there is a reasonable chance I'll still be building little websites and applications long after anyone has stopped asking me to. I'd just like to be able to choose what I work on, and when.

I can picture a fairly ordinary Wednesday in Malta. I'd go out for coffee, perhaps at a local café or in the piazza in Mosta with my parents. Later I might go and check on an Airbnb apartment because guests are arriving at four, then meet a friend for lunch while they're on a break from work. I'd spend part of the afternoon sending my sixth email to Apple, explaining why they really should allow FieldEric onto the App Store. The precise number of emails is speculative, but the prospect of arguing with Apple about a small software project seems entirely realistic.

In the evening, Frederika and I could have a beer somewhere overlooking the sea and discuss our next trip to Slovakia. Then dinner at home in the garden, with family around, and a bottle of Maltese wine. It's not an especially ambitious picture of financial independence, but I can't think of many ways I'd rather spend a Wednesday. We'd still have responsibilities, and I'm sure I'd manage to invent more for myself. They just wouldn't have to take up the whole day.

There are things I'd miss about Seattle. The air, for one. The greenery and the ability to be fairly anonymous are nice too. And Amazon deliveries, which is a less poetic answer but probably the first one that comes to mind. I've grown used to online services that work without requiring me to make a phone call. In Malta, trying to sort out something with a bank or a government office can still involve speaking to three people and receiving three entirely different answers. I have no reason to believe I'll suddenly become more patient with that.

I also don't expect that living in Malta will feel like a permanent holiday. I'll complain about traffic, construction and bureaucracy, and eventually some local inconvenience will make me wonder why I ever left Seattle's fresh air and working websites. But we'll be near family and old friends, with the sea close by and much of Europe a short flight away. That sounds like a good trade to me, particularly now.

As for the five years, we stopped saying it partly because it had become ridiculous and partly because we've finally admitted we don't know. We want to go back as soon as we can, but "as soon as we can" depends on reaching a level of financial security we're still trying to define. It's a less satisfying answer when someone asks for a date, but at least it's an honest one.

I'm pleased we left Malta. I loved living in England, and I'm glad we came to America. I wouldn't change those years, even if I sometimes find myself thinking about the ones that went on without me back home. There is still plenty I like about the life we have here, and quite a lot I look forward to about the one we might have there. For now, if you ask when we're moving back, I won't say five years. Given my track record, that probably counts as progress.
