Five More Years

November 2026

I left Malta almost twenty years ago, when I was 23 or 24. Looking back, I'm not sure I fully understood why I wanted to leave. Malta is tiny, and I suppose I wanted to see a bit more of the world, although at the time my experience of the world was rather limited. My first trip outside Malta was a day trip to Sicily in my early twenties. A year later, I boarded my first aeroplane, on a trip to Spain with my brother. Not exactly a seasoned traveller.

There were academic ambitions too. I'd spent two years studying electrical engineering before deciding it wasn't for me and switching to computing. That turned out to be a very good decision. I did well at university, partly because I was a little older and more mature the second time around, and partly because I had a lecturer who genuinely believed in me. He eventually became one of my closest friends. I wanted to do a master's degree and perhaps eventually a PhD. I wasn't particularly concerned with where that would take me. I just knew I wanted to do more.

America was barely even a dream back then, although I had family in the San Francisco Bay Area, so the thought occasionally crossed my mind. I ended up in England, where I spent six years before moving to Seattle. By then, I'd grown tired of London and was ready for something different. If Seattle hadn't worked out, we'd have gone somewhere else. Vienna, perhaps. Or Bangkok, of all places. I was actually interviewing with a travel company there, and they'd offered to fly me over to meet the team. I never went.

America had always held a certain fascination for me. The promised land, perhaps, or at least somewhere that seemed full of possibilities. The original plan was actually to settle in San Francisco. Seattle was supposed to be a convenient first stop, since transferring here with Expedia was relatively straightforward. But we built a life in Seattle. We made friends, established careers, and eventually realised we didn't particularly fancy starting from scratch in another city. So we stayed.

When we moved to America, we planned to stay for five years. We'd already spent six years away from family and friends in the UK, and didn't want to stay away too much longer, but we also wanted to make the move worthwhile. Five years seemed like a reasonable compromise. Whenever someone asked when we were going back, the answer was always the same. Five years. A few years passed, and it was still five years.

Then, five years ago, we bought a house in Seattle. I remember telling Frederika that five years after buying the house, we'd definitely be going back. Well, here we are, and we've stopped saying five years.

I remember talking to my brother when I was about 35. I told him we were planning to return to Malta in five years. "Shit, you'd be 40," he said. I hadn't really thought about it like that. I'm 43 now.

There's something strange about leaving home in your early twenties. In your mind, the people and places you leave behind seem to stay more or less as they were. My parents were in their fifties when I left. They're in their seventies now. My friends were teenagers or barely into their twenties. Now they're in their forties, and most have children of their own.

Of course, I know life doesn't stop just because I'm somewhere else. We've kept in touch, visited, celebrated things together when we could. But there's a difference between visiting someone's life and being part of its ordinary routine. I've missed birthdays, dinners, spontaneous coffees, and countless completely unremarkable afternoons. The sort of things nobody thinks much about at the time, but which collectively make up a life.

I sometimes wonder what mine would have looked like if I'd stayed. Would I have pursued the same career? Would I have been happier? Would I have become a different person? Perhaps. But I suspect I'd still be largely the same person, just with a different collection of experiences and stories. And I wouldn't change what I've done. Not for a moment.

A rather amusing realisation I had recently is that I'm not entirely sure I speak either of my languages properly anymore. I've spent almost twenty years living in English-speaking countries. My English is fluent, but I still have an accent, and there are times when my vocabulary or grammar reminds me that it's not my native language. Meanwhile, I left Malta at 23 or 24, and sometimes I wonder whether my Maltese is still that of a 24-year-old. Perhaps I'm linguistically stuck somewhere in my twenties, while the rest of me has stubbornly continued ageing.

In reality, I think quite comfortably in both languages. But it's an odd feeling, having spent so much of your adult life away from the place where you grew up. And Malta itself has changed enormously. Whenever I visit, it immediately feels like home. I've never felt like a tourist there. But it's not quite the Malta I remember.

It's busier, more crowded, and, in some ways, less Maltese. You can barely order a fucking pastizz in Maltese anymore. The construction is relentless, with cranes everywhere you look. Towns I remember have been transformed, and much of the little countryside we had seems to have disappeared under concrete. The politics still drive me mad. And yet, plenty has improved too. There are better restaurants, nicer bars, and considerably more interesting places to spend an evening.

Admittedly, I also have a little more money to enjoy them these days. When I lived in Malta, I sometimes put just enough petrol in the car to make it through the rest of the week, if I was lucky. Perhaps that changes your appreciation of a nice restaurant.

Frederika has a similar story, although hers took a rather different turn. She left Slovakia shortly after graduating in mechanical engineering, just as the 2008 financial crisis was making it a particularly unfortunate time to be looking for work. She found a job as a cashier at Zara. From there, through determination and hard work, she gradually built an entirely different career. Today she works in financial leadership, and she genuinely loves what she does. She loves accounting. She'll happily talk about it for hours. I don't think I've ever heard her talk about mechanical engineering with quite the same enthusiasm.

Interestingly, she'd studied accounting before switching to engineering. Somehow, after travelling abroad and taking an unexpected detour through retail, she found her way back to it. But I sometimes wonder what would have happened if she'd stayed in Slovakia and found a job as an engineer. Perhaps she'd have loved that too. When she first moved abroad, her English might not have been good enough to talk about engineering in any great depth. By the time it was, she'd been away from the subject for years. Perhaps that's why I've never heard her talk about engineering the way she talks about finance. Or perhaps she'd have ended up in finance regardless.

We'll never know, and that's something I've come to appreciate about the choices we make. We only get to live one version of our lives. We can imagine the alternatives, but we can't really know what they would have been like. I'm quite happy with the version we've lived.

For a long time, returning to Malta was just something we'd do in five years. Now, strangely enough, I'm more certain than ever that I want to go back, but less certain about when. The problem is that I don't particularly want to return to Malta to work a conventional nine-to-five job. I don't mean that in any superior or dismissive way. It's simply that after spending twenty years building careers abroad, and missing so much time with family and friends, I'd like the next chapter to be a little different.

I'd like to have enough financial independence to decide how I spend my days. And the funny thing is, I don't even know exactly what financial independence means for us yet. I don't necessarily want to retire. I enjoy building software, and I doubt I'll ever stop coming up with ideas for things that probably don't need to exist. I'd just like to work on them because I want to, rather than because I have to.

When I imagine an ordinary Wednesday in Malta, it's not particularly extravagant. Maybe I'd start the morning with a coffee at a local café, or meet my parents in the piazza in Mosta. Later, I'd check on an Airbnb rental because some guests are arriving at four. Perhaps I'd meet an old friend for lunch during their break from work. In the afternoon, I'd finish writing my sixth email to Apple, trying to convince them to approve my FieldEric app for the App Store. Some things, apparently, will never be easy.

Then a beer with Frederika somewhere overlooking the sea, discussing our next trip to Slovakia. Maybe dinner at home in the garden, a nice bottle of Maltese wine, and family around the table. That's about it. Not exactly the lifestyle of the rich and famous, but it sounds rather wonderful to me.

Of course, there are things I'd miss about Seattle. Amazon deliveries, for starters. Seriously, the convenience of life here is difficult to overstate. So many things just work. In Malta, there's a good chance that sorting out something with a bank or government office will involve several phone calls, three different people, and three completely different answers. I'd miss Seattle's fresh air, the greenery, and perhaps even the anonymity of living somewhere nobody particularly cares what you're doing.

And I know perfectly well that moving back to Malta won't magically remove life's frustrations. I'll still complain about traffic, construction, bureaucracy, and probably a dozen things I've forgotten about after twenty years away. There's every chance we'll spend a few years there and look at each other wondering what the fuck we're doing. We've been away for a long time, and perhaps the Malta we want to live in isn't quite the Malta that exists today.

If that happens, I don't think we'd come back to America. We're planning to get US citizenship, partly because it's good to have the option, but I suspect we'd stay somewhere in Europe. I've even warmed to the idea of Košice, which I would have dismissed a few years ago. We genuinely enjoyed our last visit. But then there's Italy, and Sicily in particular. Wonderful food and wine, beautiful countryside, beaches, sunshine, and people whose way of life I've always loved. All a stone's throw from Malta. It would be hard to justify moving somewhere else without at least giving that serious consideration.

Of course, perhaps we'll move back to Malta and never want to leave. I rather hope that's how it turns out. But if the last twenty years have taught me anything, it's that our plans have a habit of changing, often for the better. We went to England, then Seattle instead of San Francisco, and somehow stayed considerably longer than expected. I'm not particularly worried about knowing exactly where we'll end up next.

So why have we stopped saying five years? Partly because it eventually started sounding ridiculous. There's only so many times you can repeat the same prediction before even you stop believing it. But mostly because we've accepted that we don't know. We want to go back as soon as we can, but we also want to go back on our own terms. We have some idea of what that life looks like. We're still figuring out what it takes to get there.

In the meantime, I'm grateful for the life we've built. England and America have given us experiences, friendships, opportunities, and perspectives that I couldn't have imagined when I first boarded that plane to Spain. I sometimes think about the years I missed in Malta, but I don't regret the years I spent elsewhere. Both feelings can be true.

And there's something rather exciting about having reached a point in life where the next big adventure might simply be going home. I don't know when that will be, but I'm looking forward to it.
